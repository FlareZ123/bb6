# Three stubborn Raw40 A1=1LA/B1=0RE leaves: unary-run grammar

**Mathematical result (paper proof; Lean integration not yet accepted):** all compatible completions of the following partial machines are nonhalting from blank. The missing entry is E0 in all three.

| Canonical leaf | Partial table |
|---|---|
| 3535 | `1RB1LA_1RC0RE_0RD0LD_1LD1LA_---1RF_1RC1LC` |
| 3594 | `1RB1LA_1RC0RE_0RD0LA_1LD1LA_---1RF_1RC1LF` |
| 3595 | `1RB1LA_1RC0RE_0RD0LD_1LD1LA_---1RF_1RC1LF` |

## Exact macro rules

At every checkpoint A scans zero. Write the left runs as a list L of positive lengths, nearest first; write the right runs R in order away from the head. Successive runs are separated by a single zero, with blank beyond the outermost runs. Define inc(L,b) to add b to its first run, or create [b] if L is empty. The checkpoint notation C(L,R) means the actual tape, not a bounded prefix. Let a be the first left length (0 if L=[]), and P the remaining left runs.

All three machines reach C([], [4]) in exactly 7 ordinary transitions. The following rules are valid with arbitrary untouched tails. Costs are ordinary transitions.

1. **Split/retreat.** If R=(k+3)::S, write the first run as 1111^k. The short variant 3535 performs 7 moves and the other two perform 9 moves, leaving state A on the cell immediately left of the original head, with right word 1101^(k+1) followed by the untouched suffix. A1 moves left over the a ones (one move each), stopping on the previous separator or blank. Thus
   C(a::P,(k+3)::S) -> C(P,(a+2)::(k+1)::S),
   and C([],(k+3)::S) -> C([],[2,k+1]++S).
   The cost is 7+a or 9+a. All executed entries are assigned.
2. **Consume.** If the right word begins 1101, exactly 7 moves transfer one one into the nearest left run and delete one one from the run following the 11. For arbitrary tails X,Y, with the scanned zero explicit, the exact zipper identity is
   run7(A,0X,1101Y)=(A,01X,110Y).
   The same identity holds with an implicit left blank, taking X empty. Repeating n>0 times from R=[2,n]++S leaves left inc(L,n) and right word 110 followed by the delimiter and runs of S (or only 110 if S=[]).
3. **Skip.** For nonempty S the preceding result has right word 1100W(S). Exactly 6 moves give
   run6(A,0X,1100Y)=(A,01101X,Y).
   So a complete consume+skip from C(L,[2,n]++S) with S nonempty reaches C(2::inc(L,n+1),S) after 7n+6 moves.
4. **End.** With right word 11 or 110 (remaining cells blank), exactly 9 moves yield right word 1111 and increment the nearest left run by 1. Thus C(L,[2]) -> C(inc(L,1),[4]); the terminal pair [2,n] takes 7n+9 moves to C(inc(L,n+1),[4]).

For rule 1, the finite zipper identities before sweeping A1 are (A,0X,111Y) -> (A,X,1101Y), in 7 or 9 moves; implicit-left-blank version has X empty. Rules 2–4 are literally common to the three tables despite different intermediate C1/F1 moves. All operations have positive cost and avoid E0 by direct inspection. These identities prove the rules for arbitrary tails; induction on a or n proves the repeated sweeps. No finite-duration extrapolation is used.

## Inductive grammar and epoch

Let G be the least set of finite lists generated by:
- [] and [2] belong to G;
- if S belongs to G and n>=1, then [2,n]++S belongs to G;
- if S belongs to G and m>=3, then m::S belongs to G.

Every nonempty member starts with a run of length at least 2. At the leftmost checkpoint the right list is a nonempty member of G. During a rightward consume/skip scan, the left list has shape [] or 2::P, where the last element of P is >=2 and every other element is >=3. Initially this holds trivially. For the first pair [2,n], the new left list is [2,n+1]; for a subsequent pair it is [2,n+3]++P. The remaining right list belongs to G because the pair is a grammar constructor.

A scan consists of finitely many pairs (each reduces the remaining list length by two), followed by one of the following:
- **Big head m>=3:** repeated split/retreat (one per left run, then one at the blank) gives C([], [2]++reverse(L)++[m-2]++S). If L=[], this is the legal pair [2,m-2] followed by S. Otherwise reverse(L)=reverse(P)++[2], and the resulting list is [2]++reverse(P)++[2,m-2]++S. The first two elements form a pair, the intervening elements of reverse(P) are all >=3, and [2,m-2] is another pair. Thus it belongs to G.
- **Terminal singleton [2] or terminal pair [2,n]:** the end rule gives C(inc(L,b),[4]), where b=1 or n+1 respectively. Repeated split/retreat returns to C([], [2]++reverse(inc(L,b))++[2]). If L=[], the result [2,b,2] is a pair and singleton. Otherwise reverse(inc(L,b))=reverse(P)++[2+b], so the first two elements form a pair, the intervening elements are >=3, and the final 2+b>=3 and singleton 2 are legal. Thus it belongs to G.

Every epoch returns to the left blank with a nonempty G list in a positive number of defined transitions. The initial list [4] belongs to G. Induction on epochs yields arbitrarily long live primitive prefixes. Any compatible completion follows these same prefixes, hence cannot halt. In particular E0 is never queried, without a global time cutoff.

## Integration

This closes the three mathematical residuals of the A1=1LA/B1=0RE discovery bank, subject to formal checker integration. The proof uses a variable-length run-list grammar rather than an affine fixed-word pump or bounded two-sided stack abstraction. The complete A1=1LA family is not yet a kernel-accepted bound.
